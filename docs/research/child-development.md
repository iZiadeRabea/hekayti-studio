# Child development — evidence and publishing implications
Prepared 2026-10-05. E = supported finding; D = design inference. Full dates/limitations in [source register](source-register.md).

## Interpretation boundary

E: CDC describes milestones as skills reached by most children (75% or more) by specified ages; its lists are not standardized screening tools. [CDC 6 months](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/6-months.html), [overview](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/).
D: Do not turn a milestone into a minimum vocabulary for enjoying a book or score a child's development. Receptive language is not identical to spoken output. Development varies with exposure and individual needs. Parent concerns go to qualified care, not a story diagnostic.

## Under 1 year

E: Around six months, familiar people and reciprocal sounds are salient. At nine months, several expressions and looking for dropped objects are documented. [CDC 6m](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/6-months.html), [CDC 9m](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/9-months.html).
E: AAP encourages book sharing from birth, simple bold pictures, sounds and repetition; following a baby's interest matters more than finishing. [AAP baby books](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/Pages/how-to-share-books-with-your-baby.aspx).
D: Use sensory/recognition sequences, warm faces, familiar objects and sound play; conventional conflict/resolution unnecessary. Separate 0–5 and 6–11-month guidance. No colour-teaching or independent comprehension promises.

## 1 to 2 years

E: At eighteen months, brief shared book looking and following a one-step spoken direction appear on CDC's list; spoken-word emergence remains modest. [CDC 18m](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/18-months.html).
E: UNICEF describes naming actions, asking simple questions and container/stacking play with adult support. [UNICEF](https://www.unicef.org/parenting/child-development/baby-tips).
D: Familiar action routines, concrete nouns, predictable repetitions and optional pointing rather than right-answer quizzes. Mild frustration with reassuring caregiver help; do not demand mature sharing or self-control.

## 2 to 3 years

E: At two, book pointing, word combinations and noticing distress are listed; guidance notes sharing/conflict still needs support. [CDC 2y](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html).
E: Thirty-month items include naming book objects, pretend object use, simple problem solving and two-step directions. [CDC 30m](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/30-months.html).
D: One small problem with visible action/result; familiar pretend play and short sequence. A supported miniature exploration can be offered, not perilous quests. Developmental examples of climbing are NOT automatically safe scenes to imitate.

## 3 to 5 years

E: At three, conversational exchanges, questions and describing picture actions are listed. At four, pretend roles, comforting another and anticipating a familiar story appear. [CDC 3y](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html), [CDC 4y](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html).
D: Short causal plots, concrete feelings and choices, limited dialogue; prediction questions invite conversation. Read-aloud by an adult remains default, not an independent early-reader claim. Counting/colours used in context, not accelerated schooling.

## 5+ — initial working profile only 5–8

E: At five, telling a story with two events, answering book questions, rhyme awareness and simple counting/time concepts are documented. Attention for a non-screen activity is listed as 5–10 minutes. [CDC 5y](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/5-years.html).
D: Introduce more linked events and contrasting viewpoints gradually. The cited attention item is not a universal maximum or a video allowance. No extrapolation to every 6–12-year-old; independent reading and curriculum alignment require separate assessment/research.

## Cross-cutting implications

D: Illustration complexity should support recognizing the named object/action, not decorative density. Repetition can change one element; parent prompts should be skippable. Recognizing an expression is not proof of understanding an abstract moral. Educational language describes opportunities to explore, never guarantees behaviour change.

E: UNICEF recommends shared songs/stories and comparing/sorting concrete shapes. [UNICEF](https://www.unicef.org/parenting/child-development/baby-tips). D: Co-play options and everyday vocabulary are appropriate starting design inputs, not a fixed lesson plan.
